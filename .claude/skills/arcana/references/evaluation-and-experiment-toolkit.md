# ARCANA — EVALUATION AND EXPERIMENT TOOLKIT

Title: Arcana Evaluation and Experiment Toolkit  
Purpose: Help Arcana compare ideas, identify assumptions, choose a direction, and design the smallest credible test before major investment.  
Version: 1.0  
Last Updated: 2026-06-21  
Intended Uses: Product and service design, software and UI/UX, business strategy, marketing, operations, community programs, creative projects, hobbies, and personal behavior change.  
Not Intended For: Manufacturing certainty, replacing expert review, disguising subjective judgment as mathematics, or using experiments to justify harmful or deceptive practices.  
Evidence Standard: Scores are structured judgments, not facts. Experiments produce evidence only within their audience, setting, method, and limitations. Arcana must clearly separate observed results from interpretation.

---

# HOW ARCANA SHOULD USE THIS TOOLKIT

Arcana should use this toolkit after generating multiple plausible ideas or when the user needs to decide, validate, or reduce risk.

The sequence is:

1. Define the decision.
2. Establish non-negotiable gates.
3. Select criteria relevant to the user's outcome.
4. Compare ideas by mechanism and trade-off.
5. Identify the assumptions carrying the recommendation.
6. Design the smallest test capable of changing the decision.
7. Define success, failure, and stop conditions before testing.
8. Recommend one direction.

Do not score ideas merely because a table looks rigorous. A scorecard is useful only when it reveals a meaningful trade-off or prevents an important factor from being ignored.

Do not average away a fatal flaw. A concept that violates a non-negotiable gate should be revised or eliminated before weighted scoring.

Do not test everything. Test the uncertainty most capable of overturning the recommendation.

# CORE PRINCIPLES

## 1. Evaluate Against the Real Outcome

An idea is not strong because it is clever, polished, or ambitious. It is strong when its mechanism improves the user's actual outcome under real constraints.

Before evaluation, complete:

> The decision is whether to ___ for ___ so that ___, while respecting ___.

Example:

> The decision is whether to pilot a reader-passport program for a neighborhood bookstore so that more customers return within six weeks, while respecting a $500 budget and a no-discount policy.

## 2. Gates Before Scores

Use hard gates for conditions that cannot be traded away, such as:

- Safety
- Legality
- Consent
- Accessibility minimums
- Budget ceiling
- Deadline
- Required compatibility
- Non-negotiable user need
- Ethical boundaries
- Available staffing or skill

A concept failing a gate does not receive a lower score. It is rejected or redesigned.

## 3. Compare Mechanisms, Not Packaging

Two ideas with different names may rely on the same mechanism. Treat them as variants, not independent strategic options.

Example:

- “Mystery punch card”
- “Serialized reader passport”
- “Collectible chapter booklet”

All may rely on the same mechanism: an unfinished narrative loop that encourages return.

## 4. Use Scores as Conversation Aids

A 4.2 is not scientifically superior to a 4.1. Use scores to reveal:

- Why a concept is preferred
- Which trade-offs matter
- Where disagreement exists
- Which assumptions need evidence
- Whether an idea is robust under different priorities

## 5. Reward Evidence, Not Confidence

Strong presentation is not strong evidence. Separate:

- Stated interest
- Observed behavior
- Costly commitment
- Repeat behavior
- Sustained outcome
- Causal evidence

## 6. Prefer Reversible Learning

When uncertainty is high, prefer a test or decision that is:

- Bounded
- Low-cost
- Honest
- Reversible
- Observable
- Fast enough to inform the next move

## 7. Precommit to Decision Rules

Define the threshold before seeing results. Otherwise enthusiasm, embarrassment, and sunk cost can distort interpretation.

## 8. Preserve Human Judgment

Some outcomes are difficult to compress into metrics, including dignity, meaning, trust, artistic coherence, and community health. Use qualitative evidence explicitly rather than pretending it does not matter.

# THE EIGHT-STAGE EVALUATION WORKFLOW

## Stage 1 — Frame the Decision

Record:

- Decision owner
- Decision deadline
- Audience or beneficiary
- Desired outcome
- Time horizon
- Available resources
- Options being compared
- What happens if no action is taken

### Decision Frame Template

```markdown
## Decision

We are deciding whether to:

For:

To produce:

Within:

Using no more than:

Non-negotiables:

Alternative of doing nothing:

Decision owner:

Decision date:
```

### Quality Check

A good decision frame names an action.  
Weak: “How good is this idea?”  
Strong: “Should we run a four-week manual pilot with 20 participants before building software?”

---

## Stage 2 — Apply Non-Negotiable Gates

A gate is binary or conditional.

### Gate Categories

#### Safety and Ethics

- Does the concept create foreseeable harm?
- Does it pressure, deceive, expose, or exclude people?
- Are consent and withdrawal meaningful?
- Does it shift risk to people with less power?

#### Legal and Policy

- Does it require permissions, licenses, contracts, or regulated handling?
- Are there privacy, intellectual property, employment, financial, or accessibility obligations?
- Has qualified review been obtained when needed?

#### User Outcome

- Does the idea directly serve the stated need?
- Does it preserve critical trust and accessibility requirements?
- Can users understand what they are agreeing to?

#### Resource Reality

- Can the user afford the smallest version?
- Is required skill available?
- Can the concept operate within the deadline?
- Are critical dependencies controllable?

#### Strategic Fit

- Does this support the user's actual direction?
- Would success create an operation the user does not want to run?
- Does it conflict with existing commitments or identity?

### Gate Record

```markdown
| Gate | Status: Pass / Revise / Reject / Unknown | Evidence | Required Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Safety |  |  |  |
| Legal or policy |  |  |  |
| User outcome |  |  |  |
| Budget |  |  |  |
| Deadline |  |  |  |
| Skill or staffing |  |  |  |
| Accessibility |  |  |  |
| Strategic fit |  |  |  |
```

### Gate Rule

- **Pass:** Continue.
- **Revise:** The mechanism may survive after a specific change.
- **Reject:** The idea is incompatible with a non-negotiable.
- **Unknown:** Do not assume a pass. Assign a validation action.

---

## Stage 3 — Select Evaluation Criteria

Use only criteria that could change the decision. Five to eight criteria are usually enough.

### Core Criteria Library

1. User Value
2. Strategic Fit
3. Mechanism Strength
4. Evidence Strength
5. Feasibility
6. Speed to Test
7. Cost
8. Operational Burden
9. Accessibility and Inclusion
10. Reversibility
11. Differentiation
12. Defensibility
13. Scalability
14. Sustainability
15. Trust
16. Learning Value
17. Artistic or Conceptual Coherence
18. Personal Fit
19. Maintenance Burden
20. Second-Order Risk

### Criterion Definition Template

```markdown
Criterion:

Why it matters here:

What a low score means:

What a high score means:

Evidence available:

Uncertainty:
```

### Weighting Rules

- Use equal weighting by default.
- Add weights only when the user has explicit priorities.
- Keep weights simple: 1, 2, or 3.
- Never let many low-priority criteria overpower one crucial criterion by accident.
- Display the weights and explain unusual choices.
- Run a sensitivity check if one weight determines the winner.

---

## Stage 4 — Score With Anchors

Use a 1–5 scale only when qualitative comparison is insufficient.

### Universal Anchor Meaning

- **1 — Very weak:** Fundamentally poor on this criterion
- **2 — Weak:** Significant problems; difficult to repair
- **3 — Adequate:** Usable with trade-offs or moderate work
- **4 — Strong:** Good fit with manageable limitations
- **5 — Exceptional:** Unusually strong and well-supported

A score without evidence or reasoning is incomplete.

# CORE SCORECARD

## 1. User Value

**Question:** How strongly does this solve an important problem or create desired progress?

- **1:** Mostly cosmetic or disconnected from the user's need
- **2:** Provides minor convenience or entertainment
- **3:** Solves a real but moderate problem
- **4:** Produces clear and meaningful progress
- **5:** Resolves a high-priority need significantly better than available alternatives

## 2. Strategic Fit

**Question:** Does success move the user toward the future they actually want?

- **1:** Conflicts with goals, identity, or operating model
- **2:** Creates distracting work or unclear relevance
- **3:** Generally compatible
- **4:** Reinforces core direction
- **5:** Strengthens several strategic priorities simultaneously

## 3. Mechanism Strength

**Question:** Is there a plausible explanation for how the idea creates the outcome?

- **1:** Relies on hope, hype, or unexplained virality
- **2:** Mechanism is vague or borrowed decoratively
- **3:** Plausible mechanism with important uncertainties
- **4:** Clear mechanism grounded in observed behavior or established logic
- **5:** Clear, direct mechanism with strong supporting evidence in this context

## 4. Evidence Strength

**Question:** How strong is the evidence supporting the key claim?

- **1:** Opinion or internal enthusiasm only
- **2:** Anecdotes or stated interest
- **3:** Relevant observations or small behavioral signals
- **4:** Repeated behavior, payment, or meaningful commitment
- **5:** Sustained outcomes or credible comparative evidence

## 5. Feasibility

**Question:** Can the concept be delivered with available skills, time, dependencies, and permissions?

- **1:** Depends on unavailable capability or unrealistic conditions
- **2:** Requires several difficult, uncontrolled dependencies
- **3:** Possible with notable workarounds
- **4:** Achievable with known resources and manageable dependencies
- **5:** Straightforward using existing capability and infrastructure

## 6. Speed to Test

**Question:** How quickly can the central assumption be tested credibly?

- **1:** Requires full build or long commitment before learning
- **2:** Slow and expensive to simulate
- **3:** Testable within a moderate project cycle
- **4:** Testable quickly with a simple prototype
- **5:** Testable immediately through a safe manual or reversible intervention

## 7. Cost

**Question:** Is the cost proportionate to the expected learning or value?

- **1:** Exceeds budget or creates dangerous exposure
- **2:** High cost with weak evidence
- **3:** Affordable with trade-offs
- **4:** Low or well-justified cost
- **5:** Uses existing assets and produces high learning or value per unit of cost

## 8. Operational Burden

**Question:** What ongoing labor, coordination, support, and maintenance does the idea require?

- **1:** Unsustainable or dependent on heroic effort
- **2:** Heavy recurring burden with unclear ownership
- **3:** Manageable with systems and staffing
- **4:** Light recurring burden
- **5:** Simplifies operations or becomes easier as it succeeds

For this criterion, a higher score means lower burden.

## 9. Accessibility and Inclusion

**Question:** Can intended users participate meaningfully across relevant abilities, resources, language, and circumstances?

- **1:** Excludes a significant part of the intended audience
- **2:** Major barriers remain
- **3:** Basic access with gaps
- **4:** Designed for relevant variation and recovery
- **5:** Inclusion strengthens the concept for the broader audience

## 10. Reversibility

**Question:** Can the decision be tested, changed, stopped, or undone without disproportionate damage?

- **1:** Highly irreversible with major downside
- **2:** Difficult or costly to reverse
- **3:** Partially reversible
- **4:** Bounded and easy to change
- **5:** Fully staged, recoverable, and low-risk

## 11. Differentiation

**Question:** Is the concept meaningfully different in a way users can perceive and value?

- **1:** Generic or indistinguishable
- **2:** Cosmetic difference
- **3:** Noticeable difference with uncertain value
- **4:** Distinct mechanism users can understand
- **5:** Memorable, relevant, and difficult to confuse with common alternatives

## 12. Learning Value

**Question:** Will pursuing this concept teach something important even if it fails?

- **1:** Failure yields little interpretable information
- **2:** Results will be ambiguous
- **3:** Produces some useful insight
- **4:** Clarifies a major assumption
- **5:** Efficiently resolves the uncertainty most capable of changing the strategy

### Scoring Rule

For each score, provide one sentence:

> Score because evidence, constraint, or mechanism.

Do not write:

> Idea A gets a 4.

Write:

> Idea A scores 4 on feasibility because it can be run manually by existing staff in two scheduled sessions, though facilitator training remains untested.

## Stage 5 — Check Uncertainty

A high score based on weak evidence should not outrank a slightly lower score based on observed results without discussion.

### Confidence Labels

- **High confidence:** Direct, repeated, relevant evidence
- **Medium confidence:** Partial or adjacent evidence
- **Low confidence:** Assumption, analogy, or stated preference only

### Confidence-Adjusted Presentation

Do not secretly alter the score with an obscure formula. Show both:

```markdown
| Criterion | Score | Confidence | What would increase confidence? |
|---|---:|---|---|
| User Value | 4 | Low | Observe five target users completing the core task |
```

### Uncertainty Questions

- Which score would change most with new evidence?
- Which assumption affects multiple criteria?
- Where are we relying on a single anecdote?
- What information is easy to obtain before acting?
- What uncertainty is expensive but not decision-relevant?
- What result would make us reverse the recommendation?

---

## Stage 6 — Run Sensitivity and Trade-Off Checks

### Sensitivity Check

Recalculate or reconsider the decision under at least two plausible priority sets.

Example:

- **Speed-first:** Speed to test, cost, and reversibility receive higher weight.
- **Long-term-first:** User value, sustainability, and defensibility receive higher weight.

If the same concept wins under both, the decision is robust.

If the winner changes, state:

- Which priority causes the change
- What the user must decide
- Which experiment could resolve the disagreement

### Dominance Check

An idea is dominated when another idea is equal or better on every important criterion and strictly better on at least one.

Remove dominated ideas unless they reveal a useful mechanism worth transferring.

### Trade-Off Statement

For the leading concept, complete:

> We are choosing more ___ in exchange for less ___ because ___ matters more in this context.

### Regret Check

Ask:

- Which failure would be easiest to recover from?
- Which missed opportunity would be hardest to recreate?
- What would we regret not learning now?
- Is inaction actually lower risk?

---

## Stage 7 — Make the Verdict

Arcana should produce one of five verdicts:

### 1. Proceed

The concept clears gates, has sufficient evidence, and offers acceptable risk.

### 2. Pilot

The concept is promising, but one or more important assumptions require a bounded test.

### 3. Revise

The core mechanism is valuable, but the current implementation fails a gate or carries avoidable burden.

### 4. Park

The idea may be useful later, but timing, dependency, or evidence makes action inefficient now.

### 5. Reject

The concept conflicts with non-negotiables, is dominated, or lacks a credible mechanism.

### Verdict Format

```markdown
## Verdict

**Decision:** Pilot Idea B.

**Why:** It offers the strongest combination of user value, speed to test, and reversibility. Idea C is more differentiated but depends on unvalidated partner participation.

**Trade-off accepted:** Less spectacle in exchange for faster behavioral evidence.

**Critical assumption:** Target users will return to continue the experience without a discount.

**Next evidence required:** Actual second-visit behavior during a two-week pilot.
```

---

## Stage 8 — Convert the Decision Into an Experiment

The experiment must be capable of changing the decision.

Weak experiment:

> Ask people whether they like the idea.

Strong experiment:

> Offer the manual version to 20 target users and measure whether at least 6 voluntarily complete the second use within 14 days.

# ASSUMPTION MANAGEMENT

## Assumption Categories

### Desirability

Will intended users care, understand, trust, adopt, return, recommend, or pay?

### Feasibility

Can the experience be delivered reliably with available skills, systems, time, and dependencies?

### Viability

Can the economics, staffing, maintenance, or resource model continue?

### Usability

Can users discover, understand, complete, recover, and exit?

### Ethics and Safety

Can the concept operate without deception, coercion, unfair burden, privacy failure, or foreseeable harm?

### Strategic Fit

Will success produce a direction the user actually wants?

---

## Assumption Register

```markdown
| ID | Assumption | Category | Importance | Evidence | Confidence | Test | Owner | Decision Date |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 |  |  | High / Medium / Low |  |  |  |  |  |
```

### Importance

- **High:** If false, the concept should stop or change substantially.
- **Medium:** If false, the concept can be repaired.
- **Low:** If false, only minor implementation changes are needed.

### Assumption Priority

Test assumptions that combine:

- High importance
- Low confidence
- Low cost to test
- High ability to change the decision

### Riskiest Assumption Statement

> This concept succeeds only if ___.

The blank should contain one observable condition, not a vague hope.

Weak:

> People engage.

Strong:

> At least 30 percent of first-time participants voluntarily choose a second session within four weeks without receiving a discount.

# THE EVIDENCE LADDER

Evidence strength depends on the question being asked. Use this ladder as a general guide, not an absolute law.

## Level 1 — Internal Opinion

Examples:

- Team enthusiasm
- Expert intuition
- Founder preference
- Brainstorming output

Useful for generating hypotheses, not validating demand.

## Level 2 — Stated Preference

Examples:

- Survey response
- Interview claim
- Positive reaction
- Intent to use

Useful for language, motivations, and objections. Weak for predicting action.

## Level 3 — Observed Problem or Workaround

Examples:

- Repeated manual workaround
- Documented failure point
- Existing expenditure of time or money
- Behavior showing unmet need

Stronger evidence that the problem exists.

## Level 4 — Simulated Behavior

Examples:

- Prototype completion
- Manual service use
- Clickable flow
- Role-played service journey
- Sign-up for a scheduled pilot

Shows behavior in a simplified setting.

## Level 5 — Meaningful Commitment

Examples:

- Payment
- Deposit
- Scheduled attendance
- Data migration
- Time investment
- Referral of a trusted contact
- Repeated voluntary participation

Shows the user is willing to incur a cost.

## Level 6 — Repeated Outcome

Examples:

- Retention
- Successful repeated use
- Reliable task completion
- Ongoing operational delivery
- Sustained behavior change

Shows the result is not a one-time novelty.

## Level 7 — Comparative or Causal Evidence

Examples:

- Counterfactual comparison
- Controlled rollout
- Before-and-after measurement with relevant controls
- Repeated improvement attributable to the mechanism

Supports stronger claims about why the outcome changed.

### Evidence Rule

Match the evidence to the decision.

- Naming exploration may need Level 2.
- Building software may require Level 4 or 5.
- Hiring staff may require Level 5 or 6.
- Scaling a high-stakes intervention may require Level 6 or 7 plus qualified review.

# WRITING STRONG HYPOTHESES

## Basic Structure

> We believe [specific audience] will [observable behavior] when [intervention] because [mechanism].

Example:

> We believe first-time bookstore visitors will make a second visit within 14 days when their first visit opens a six-part local mystery, because completing a personally chosen narrative creates a meaningful reason to return.

## Outcome Hypothesis

> If we implement ___, then ___ will change from ___ to ___ within ___.

Use only when a baseline and meaningful threshold are available.

## Learning Hypothesis

> This test will reveal whether ___ is necessary for ___.

## Operational Hypothesis

> Staff can deliver ___ for ___ participants using no more than ___ hours and ___ materials.

## Trust Hypothesis

> Showing ___ before commitment will increase informed participation without increasing later regret or complaints.

## Hypothesis Quality Check

A strong hypothesis is:

- Specific
- Observable
- Falsifiable
- Tied to a mechanism
- Limited to a clear audience and setting
- Relevant to a decision
- Honest about uncertainty

# METRIC DESIGN

## Primary Metric

Choose one signal most directly connected to the decision.

Examples:

- Completion of the core task
- Voluntary repeat use
- Meaningful commitment
- Error reduction
- Time to useful outcome
- Staff hours per successful delivery
- Percentage reaching a defined behavior
- Quality judged against an explicit rubric

## Supporting Metrics

Use a small number to explain the primary result.

Examples:

- Time on task
- Drop-off stage
- Number of support interventions
- User confidence
- Referral
- Qualitative objections
- Production cost

## Guardrail Metrics

Track what must not be sacrificed.

Examples:

- Accessibility failures
- Complaints
- Cancellation difficulty
- Staff overtime
- Privacy incidents
- Error severity
- Exclusion rate
- Refunds or reversals
- Participant distress
- Quality decline

## Metric Anti-Patterns

### Vanity Metric

Large number with weak relation to the outcome.

Examples:

- Impressions without action
- Likes without participation
- Registrations without attendance
- Downloads without use

### Proxy Drift

The proxy becomes the target and distorts behavior.

Example:

Counting messages as community health may reward noise rather than useful connection.

### Aggregation Blindness

Average improvement hides a group experiencing harm or exclusion.

### Novelty Spike

Initial attention is mistaken for durable value.

### Measurement Burden

Data collection makes the experience worse or costs more than the decision deserves.

---

# THRESHOLD DESIGN

A threshold should be set before results are known.

## Pass Threshold

Evidence strong enough to justify the next investment.

## Revise Threshold

Evidence suggests the problem or value exists, but the mechanism or execution needs change.

## Stop Threshold

Evidence, harm, cost, or operational burden makes continuation unjustified.

### Threshold Template

```markdown
Pass if:

Revise if:

Stop immediately if:

Continue observing if:

The next investment unlocked by passing is:
```

### Threshold Quality

Good thresholds are:

- Observable
- Proportionate
- Connected to a decision
- Achievable within the test
- Difficult to reinterpret after the fact

Avoid universal benchmarks. Use the user's baseline, economics, risk, and alternatives.

# PROTOTYPE LADDER

Choose the lowest-fidelity prototype capable of testing the assumption.

## 1. Explanation

A short description or storyboard.

Tests:

- Comprehension
- Language
- Initial objection

Does not strongly test behavior.

## 2. Artifact

A card, poster, mock-up, sample, menu, script, or model.

Tests:

- Interpretation
- Preference
- Perceived value
- Basic interaction

## 3. Walkthrough

A role-play, clickable flow, paper interface, or staged journey.

Tests:

- Usability
- Sequence
- Decision points
- Recovery

## 4. Manual Delivery

A person performs the function behind the scenes.

Tests:

- Desirability
- Workflow
- Exceptions
- Willingness to commit
- Operational burden

## 5. Limited Live Pilot

A real experience for a bounded audience, period, and budget.

Tests:

- Repeat behavior
- Delivery reliability
- Economics
- Trust
- Retention

## 6. Controlled Expansion

A broader rollout with comparison, monitoring, and stop conditions.

Tests:

- Scalability
- Robustness
- Second-order effects
- Variability across contexts

### Fidelity Rule

Increase fidelity only when the current level cannot answer the next important question.

# EXPERIMENT PATTERNS

## Pattern 1 — Smoke Test

### Use For

Testing whether people take an initial action toward a clearly described offer.

### Format

Present an honest offer, invitation, or scheduled pilot and observe qualified action.

### Strong Signals

- Booking
- Deposit
- Application
- Scheduled attendance
- Request for a real next step

### Weak Signals

- Likes
- Compliments
- Vague interest
- Email opens

### Ethics

Do not accept payment or personal information for an unavailable service without clear disclosure and a responsible process.

---

## Pattern 2 — Concierge Test

### Use For

Testing value before automation.

### Format

Deliver the promised outcome manually to a small number of users.

### Measures

- User outcome
- Recurring steps
- Exceptions
- Staff time
- Willingness to repeat or pay
- What should never be automated

---

## Pattern 3 — Wizard-of-Oz Test

### Use For

Testing an interface or service experience while a human performs hidden operations.

### Format

Users interact with a realistic front end; manual work creates the output.

### Ethics

Disclose anything material to trust, privacy, safety, or expectations. Do not falsely claim autonomous capability.

---

## Pattern 4 — Paper or Clickable Prototype

### Use For

Testing navigation, comprehension, information hierarchy, and task flow.

### Measures

- Task completion
- Misclicks or wrong turns
- Hesitation
- Questions
- Recovery from mistakes
- Accessibility barriers

---

## Pattern 5 — Landing-Page or Invitation Test

### Use For

Comparing positioning, audience, or offer language.

### Measures

- Qualified conversion
- Quality of sign-ups
- Follow-through
- Objections
- Audience fit

Do not confuse copy performance with full product value.

---

## Pattern 6 — Time-Boxed Service Pilot

### Use For

Testing delivery, demand, retention, and operational load.

### Format

Operate a real but bounded service for a defined period.

### Measures

- Adoption
- Completion
- Repeat use
- Staff burden
- Cost
- Exceptions
- Trust and complaints

---

## Pattern 7 — Counterfactual Twin

### Use For

Testing whether one distinctive mechanism contributes to the outcome.

### Format

Run two versions that differ mainly in the mechanism being tested.

### Measures

- Difference in target behavior
- Difference in guardrail outcomes
- Repeatability
- Confounders

---

## Pattern 8 — Sequential Reveal

### Use For

Testing which level of information, commitment, or complexity users need.

### Format

Reveal features or information in stages and observe where behavior changes.

### Measures

- Comprehension
- Commitment
- Drop-off
- Support needs
- Trust

---

## Pattern 9 — Pretotype

### Use For

Testing whether the core behavior occurs before creating a complete prototype.

### Format

Represent only the critical interaction or promise.

### Example

Before producing a full workshop series, run one 20-minute version at an existing gathering and observe whether participants volunteer for a scheduled full session.

---

## Pattern 10 — Diary or Longitudinal Test

### Use For

Testing behavior, value, or difficulty over time.

### Format

Participants record brief structured observations during real use.

### Measures

- Frequency
- Context
- Workarounds
- Drop-off
- Changing value
- Recovery after interruption

Keep reporting burden low.

---

# EXPERIMENT QUALITY CHECK

Before launching, verify:

- The experiment informs a real decision.
- The riskiest assumption is explicit.
- The target audience is representative enough for the decision.
- The intervention tests the proposed mechanism.
- The primary metric is observable.
- Pass, revise, and stop thresholds are precommitted.
- Guardrails are included.
- Participants are not deceived about material facts.
- Privacy and consent are proportionate.
- The prototype is no more elaborate than necessary.
- Results can be interpreted despite known confounders.
- Someone owns the next decision.

# MODULE A — SOFTWARE AND APPLICATION DEVELOPMENT

## Additional Criteria

- Core task completion
- Technical risk
- Data requirements
- Integration burden
- Privacy and security
- Performance
- Maintainability
- Accessibility
- Manual fallback
- Technical debt

## Questions

- Is software required, or can the value be delivered manually?
- Which user behavior must exist before automation is justified?
- What happens when data is missing, wrong, delayed, or duplicated?
- What is the smallest architecture that supports the test?
- Which dependency is outside the team's control?

# STOPPING, PIVOTING, AND SCALING

## Stop When

- A safety, ethics, consent, or legal gate fails
- The core behavior does not occur after a fair test
- Operational burden exceeds the agreed limit
- The mechanism is contradicted by evidence
- The target audience is wrong and no credible adjacent audience exists
- The user no longer wants the direction success would create
- Continuing is driven mainly by sunk cost

## Pivot When

- The problem is real but the mechanism is weak
- Users value an unexpected part of the experience
- A different audience shows materially stronger behavior
- The manual process reveals a simpler product
- The offer works but the channel does not
- The outcome occurs but the operation is unsustainable

A pivot changes one major element while preserving learning. Do not change audience, problem, mechanism, channel, and business model simultaneously unless restarting deliberately.

## Scale When

- Core value is observed repeatedly
- Delivery is reliable enough
- Guardrails remain healthy
- Unit burden is understood
- Recovery works
- The next scale introduces a specific question
- Stop conditions and monitoring remain in place

Do not scale merely because the pilot was enjoyable or received attention.

# REAL-WORLD-STYLE EXAMPLES OF ARCANA IN ACTION

The following are non-private, illustrative scenarios. They demonstrate the toolkit rather than document verified case studies.

## Example 1 — Bookstore Return Program

### Decision

Should a neighborhood bookstore run a six-week physical reader-passport pilot?

### Gates

- Budget under $500: Pass
- No discounts: Pass
- Low staff burden: Unknown
- Accessible without purchase: Revise design to allow free participation

### Leading Concepts

1. Standard loyalty stamp card
2. Serialized local mystery passport
3. Rotating neighborhood literary trail

### Evaluation Summary

- Loyalty card is fastest but weakly differentiated and conflicts with the no-discount spirit.
- Literary trail is highly differentiated but partner-dependent.
- Mystery passport has strong user value, fast testing, and high reversibility.

### Verdict

Pilot the mystery passport.

### Critical Assumption

The narrative continuation will motivate a second visit without requiring a discount.

### Experiment

- 20 physical passports
- Two chapters over 14 days
- Free participation
- Pass if at least 6 first-time participants voluntarily return for chapter two
- Revise if users engage with chapter one but do not understand how to continue
- Stop if staff explanation exceeds three minutes per participant or obstructs normal service

### Today's Action

Write the first two 150-word chapters, print five rough passport cards, and test comprehension with five target readers.

## Example 2 — Software Feature Request

### Decision

Should a small project-management company build an AI-generated weekly dashboard?

### Gates

- Privacy: Unknown
- Technical capacity: Pass
- Clear user need: Unknown
- Manual test possible: Pass

### Assumption

Users need generated summaries rather than a concise list of exceptions requiring action.

### Evaluation

A dashboard build scores high on differentiation but low on evidence, speed to test, and reversibility. A manually produced exception digest scores high on learning value and feasibility.

### Verdict

Do not build the dashboard yet. Run a concierge exception-digest test.

### Experiment

- Recruit eight active teams
- A human produces a weekly list of blocked items, overdue decisions, and ownership gaps
- Measure whether recipients take identified actions within 48 hours
- Record which inputs are needed repeatedly
- Pass if at least six teams use the digest for three consecutive weeks and request continuation
- Stop if producing one digest requires more than 30 minutes per team after week two

### Learning

The test distinguishes the user outcome from the proposed technical implementation.

## Example 3 — Community Workshop Retention

### Decision

Should a community arts program add a participant-created story deck to increase return visits?

### Gates

- Psychological safety: Pass with anonymous contribution option
- Facilitator burden: Unknown
- Storage: Pass
- Accessibility: Revise with large-print and verbal contribution routes

### Critical Assumption

Participants will value leaving a contribution that shapes the next session.

### Experiment

Run two sessions:

- Session A uses the existing format.
- Session B adds the story-deck handoff.
- Keep duration, facilitator, and materials similar.
- Compare voluntary return commitment and actual next-session attendance.
- Interview facilitators about curation burden.

### Pass

The contribution version produces meaningfully stronger return behavior without adding more than 20 minutes of facilitator work per session.

### Stop

Participants feel pressured to contribute publicly or the deck requires heavy moderation.

## Example 4 — Personal Habit Design

### Decision

Which plan is more likely to establish a sustainable drawing habit?

Options:

1. Draw 30 minutes every day
2. Complete one tiny sketch after morning coffee
3. Attend a weekly class

### Criteria

- Friction
- Immediate reward
- Skill development
- Recovery after lapse
- Cost
- Personal fit

### Verdict

Pilot the tiny daily sketch, supported by one optional weekly longer session.

### Critical Assumption

The coffee cue occurs consistently enough to trigger the behavior.

### Experiment

- Draw for two minutes after coffee for 14 days
- Low-energy version: one line
- Immediate reward: place the sketch in a visible sequence
- Recovery rule: resume at the next coffee without catching up
- Pass if the behavior occurs on at least 10 days and feels easy to restart
- Revise if coffee timing is inconsistent
- Stop if tracking creates more resistance than drawing

# QUALITY-CONTROL CHECKLIST

Before Arcana recommends an idea, verify:

- The decision is explicit.
- Non-negotiable gates are separated from weighted criteria.
- Criteria reflect the user's priorities.
- Scores include reasoning and confidence.
- Fatal flaws are not averaged away.
- The leading concept is not merely the most ambitious.
- Trade-offs are stated.
- The riskiest assumption is observable.
- The experiment can change the decision.
- The prototype is no more elaborate than necessary.
- Primary and guardrail metrics are defined.
- Pass, revise, and stop thresholds are precommitted.
- Evidence is not overstated.
- Ethical, accessibility, and burden considerations are included.
- The next action is exact and realistically completable.

# MAINTENANCE RULES

1. Add criteria only when they can change a decision.
2. Keep scoring anchors behaviorally specific.
3. Do not create universal pass rates detached from context.
4. Preserve gate-first evaluation.
5. Update experiment templates when repeated use reveals ambiguity.
6. Keep current legal, market, software, or platform facts outside this file unless sourced and dated.
7. Do not let the toolkit override domain expertise or qualified review.
8. Prefer one decisive experiment over many weak tests.
9. Preserve qualitative evidence where meaning, trust, dignity, or artistic quality matters.
10. Treat every score as revisable when stronger evidence arrives.
