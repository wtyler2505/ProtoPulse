# Treat outside work as inspiration and evidence, not opposition

## What to Do

When researching or discussing work outside ProtoPulse, treat it as a source of knowledge and creative material. This includes established products, small projects, day-one repositories, papers, maker builds, forum posts, social-media ideas, issue threads, and working code.

Extract what each source can teach: a useful mechanism, a user expectation, a failure mode, a question worth testing, a reusable technique, or ingredients that could be combined into something new. Label the strength of the source honestly. Official documentation, upstream claims, reproduced results, community signals, and raw inspiration do not carry the same evidentiary weight.

Frame the result around what ProtoPulse can learn, adapt, verify, combine, or deliberately decline. Preserve direct sources and evidence labels so a future agent can tell the difference between a proven fact and an interesting spark.

## What to Avoid

Do not organize ordinary product analysis around "competitors," "threats," "moats," races, parity, catching up, leapfrogging, beating, or surpassing other people and projects. That framing turns learning into scorekeeping and makes outside work look like opposition when its actual value is what it can teach.

Do not flatten all sources into equal authority. A popular product can still teach the wrong lesson; a one-day repository or social post can offer a brilliant idea without proving that it works. Do not copy blindly, ignore licenses, weaken hardware verification, or promote an outside claim to a ProtoPulse fact without current evidence.

## Why This Matters

Tyler corrected the framing directly: he is not trying to compete with anyone. He wants "the others" — large, small, popular, brand new, or merely seen on social media — used as inspiration and as sources of knowledge and creativity. The analytical vocabulary should serve that purpose instead of quietly reinstalling a race he rejected.

## Scope

This applies to product analysis, research, roadmaps, design discussion, knowledge notes, feature ideation, and everyday language about outside work. It does not forbid a factual market comparison when price, compatibility, licensing, interoperability, or user migration genuinely requires one. Even then, the purpose is to understand the environment and make a sound decision, not to manufacture an opponent.
